A change in memory is unsettling. It is also common, and it has many causes. Some are treatable. Some are part of normal ageing. Some need a specialist. The first question most families ask is simple: who do we call?

## Start with the primary care clinician

Your primary care doctor or nurse practitioner knows your history. They can rule out the common, fixable causes first:

- Thyroid problems, low vitamin B12, and other lab findings
- Medications that cloud thinking, especially sleep aids and some allergy drugs
- Depression, which can look a lot like memory loss
- Hearing and vision loss, which make it harder to take in and recall information
- Sleep apnea and poor sleep

A short screening test in the office, such as the MoCA or the Mini-Cog, gives a rough picture. It is a starting point, not a diagnosis.

## What a primary care visit usually cannot do

A standard visit is short. It rarely includes a detailed cognitive assessment, brain imaging read by a specialist, or a plan for the family. If the screening is borderline, or if symptoms keep progressing, the next step is a referral.

## When a specialist makes sense

Ask for a neurologist or a memory clinic if any of these apply:

1. Memory or thinking has changed over months, not days.
2. The change affects daily life: bills, medications, directions, cooking.
3. There are other symptoms, such as changes in walking, mood, language or sleep behaviour.
4. The person is under 65.
5. You want to know whether newer treatments for early Alzheimer's disease are an option.

A specialist visit should include a longer history from the patient and from someone who knows them well, a full cognitive assessment, a review of imaging, and a plain conversation about what the findings mean and what happens next.

## What to bring

- A list of medications, including supplements
- A timeline: when did you first notice something, and what has changed since
- Examples. "She repeated the same question four times at dinner" helps more than "her memory is worse"
- Someone who knows the person well

## The short version

See your primary care clinician first for the basics. Ask for a specialist if the change is progressing, affects daily life, or you want a clear diagnosis and plan. You do not need to wait for a crisis to ask.
